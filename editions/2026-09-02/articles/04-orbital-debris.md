There are roughly forty thousand tracked objects in low earth orbit and some
hundreds of millions that are too small to track and entirely capable of ending
a mission. The tracked population is a catalogue. The untracked population is a
weather system.

What makes the problem hard is not the count but the derivative. Every collision
produces fragments, and every fragment is itself a collider, so the debris
population has a growth term proportional to the square of its own density. That
is the Kessler condition, and it is the same arithmetic that governs epidemics
and nuclear chain reactions: below a threshold the system decays, above it the
system runs.

## The uncomfortable part

Mitigation measures — deorbit budgets, passivation of spent stages, twenty-five
year disposal rules — all act on the *linear* term. They reduce how much new
material enters the system. They do essentially nothing to the quadratic term,
which is driven by what is already up there.

- A perfectly enforced launch-hygiene regime still leaves the existing
  population intact.
- The existing population is concentrated in exactly the bands that are most
  commercially useful.
- Fragments from a single break-up event disperse across a shell, not a track,
  so one event degrades an entire altitude for decades.

The only interventions that touch the quadratic term are removal missions, and
removal is expensive per object in a way that scales badly. Nobody has yet
demonstrated a removal architecture whose cost per kilogram is low enough to
matter against a population measured in thousands of tonnes.

## What would actually change the picture

Atmospheric drag is the one force doing useful work for free, and it is strongly
altitude-dependent. Below about 500 kilometres, debris clears itself on a
timescale of years. Above 800, the clearing timescale exceeds a century. The
practical consequence is that the altitude band between those numbers is where
policy either succeeds or fails, and it is precisely the band that the large
constellations have chosen.

This is not an argument against the constellations. It is an argument that the
disposal commitment is the whole product, and should be regulated as though it
were, rather than treated as a footnote to a spectrum licence.
