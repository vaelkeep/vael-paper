The selling began quietly, the way it usually does. By the time the morning
auction had cleared there was already a half-point of give in the ten-year, and
by noon the equity desks had stopped pretending it was a rotation.

Nothing in the data explained it. The releases had come in close enough to
consensus that the economists on the morning calls spent most of their time
apologising for having nothing to say. What moved was the story people tell
themselves about the next two quarters, and that story had been quietly
rewritten over the weekend by nobody in particular.

> The market does not price information. It prices the confidence with which
> other people hold their positions.

That is the uncomfortable part. A repricing of this shape does not need a
catalyst; it needs only a critical mass of participants deciding, more or less
simultaneously, that they would rather be smaller. The mechanism is social, and
the instrument through which it expresses itself happens to be the curve.

Three things are worth watching from here. The first is whether the long end
keeps leading — if it does, this is a term-premium story and not a policy one,
which would be the benign reading. The second is credit, which has so far
declined to participate and whose silence is either reassuring or lagging. The
third is the front end, which has barely moved at all, and whose stillness is
the strongest evidence that nobody actually believes anything has changed about
the near-term path.

There is a fourth thing, which is harder to watch because it does not print.
Positioning going into this week was, by every survey that bothers to ask, more
crowded than it has been since the spring. Crowded positioning does not cause
declines. It determines how far they run once they start, which is a different
property and a more dangerous one.

The desks that did well today were the ones that had spent August being bored.
They had taken risk down not because they saw this coming but because the
carry had stopped compensating them for holding it, and boredom turns out to be
an underrated risk-management discipline. The desks that did badly had spent
August explaining why the carry was still fine.

By the close the tape had steadied, in the way that tapes do when the sellers
have finished rather than when the buyers have arrived. That distinction will
matter tomorrow. Volume was heavy into the last twenty minutes and thin
everywhere else, which is the signature of rebalancing rather than conviction,
and it argues for treating today's levels as provisional.

None of this is a forecast. It is a description of a market that has spent four
months being told a single coherent story and has just discovered that the
story was load-bearing.
