The catalogue raisonné is a strange form: an attempt to fix, permanently and
exhaustively, a body of work that its maker experienced as provisional. Every
entry is a small act of violence against the artist's own sense that the thing
was not finished, merely abandoned.

And yet the impulse behind it is not scholarly vanity. It is closer to grief.
The catalogue appears when the work has stopped, and its exhaustiveness is a way
of insisting that the stopping did not diminish what came before.

The best of them know this and admit it. They include the failures, the
overpainted canvases, the studies that went nowhere, and in doing so they
describe a working life rather than a highlight reel. The worst of them are
sales documents with footnotes.

What they share is a faith that enumeration is a form of understanding — that if
you list a thing completely enough, its shape will emerge from the list. It is
the same faith that animates the index, the concordance, and the well-kept
notebook, and it is wrong often enough to be interesting and right often enough
to be worth the effort.
