The picture is from your own photo stock, taken at seven this morning on the
deck, and it records the correspondent at full volume. The subject was not the
photographer. It was the neighbour's tabby, who had come as far as the fence
and stopped there, which is precisely the distance Marmalade finds most
offensive.

He is, for the record, not in pain and not ill. He ate at six, used the tray,
and was on the study chair by eight, asleep, which is the arrangement he adopts
before rain and — this has been checked against the notes — at no other time.
The forecast on page one gives even odds a little after five. He gives it
rather better than that.

What the photograph shows is a cat who has been startled and then crowded, and
the household has a short procedure for that, assembled over three years and
written down here so nobody has to remember it at seven in the morning.

### Calming him down

- **Give him the room.** Step back, sit down if you can, and let him decide
  the distance. A cat who is being approached cannot also be calming down.
- **Lower your voice and your eyes.** Speak quietly, or not at all, and do not
  stare. Look away, then blink slowly at him; he usually blinks back within a
  minute, which is the moment the argument ends.
- **Do not pick him up.** Not to comfort him and not to move him. That is how
  the scratch on the back of your hand happened in March.
- **Remove the cause, not the cat.** Close the blind if the tabby is at the
  fence, switch off the vacuum, shut the door on the delivery. He will
  come down on his own once the thing is gone.
- **Offer a way out.** The study door open, the chair free. He goes there to
  end a quarrel, and he should always be able to.
- **Wait before treats.** Ten minutes of quiet first, then the wand toy for a
  few minutes if he wants it, then food. Treats during the shouting teach the
  shouting.
- **Note the time.** If a temper lasts more than an hour, or comes with hiding,
  going off food, or crying in the tray, it stops being weather and becomes an
  appointment. The vet on Eighth Street has a slot most afternoons.

There is no news in any of this. It is included because a paper for one reader
may carry one item that is only true in one house, and because the household
has now had the same argument with the same tabby on nine mornings since May.
